How to Choose the Right Print Method for Your Custom Products

By Daniel Brooks | Updated: 2026-08-01

Short answer: DTG printing suits detailed cotton t-shirt designs, sublimation suits full-coverage polyester items, screen printing wins for large batches, and laser engraving is the choice for glass and metal.

Bottom line: match the method to the product material first and the design second, because the wrong method pairing, such as sublimation on cotton, fails regardless of design quality.

July 8, 2024 | By CustomGiftLab Team

Understanding different print methods is essential for creating high-quality custom products. Each technique has unique strengths, and choosing the right one affects the look, feel, and durability of your personalized gifts.

1. Direct-to-Garment (DTG) Printing

DTG printing works like an inkjet printer for fabric. It excels at detailed, full-color designs on cotton t-shirts and apparel. The ink bonds with fibers for soft, breathable prints that withstand washing. DTG is ideal for small batches and complex artwork with gradients or photographs.

2. Sublimation Printing

Sublimation uses heat to transfer dye into polyester or polymer-coated surfaces. It produces vibrant, all-over prints that never crack or peel. This method is perfect for custom mugs, polyester shirts, phone cases, and aluminum products. The print becomes part of the material itself.

3. Screen Printing

Screen printing pushes ink through a mesh stencil onto the product. It is the most cost-effective method for large orders with simple designs. Each color requires a separate screen, making it best for designs with limited colors. The results are durable and vibrant on fabric and paper.

4. Heat Transfer Vinyl (HTV)

HTV involves cutting designs from colored vinyl sheets and heat-pressing them onto products. It works well for names, numbers, and simple graphics on t-shirts, bags, and hats. The raised texture adds a premium feel, and it is excellent for small personalization jobs.

5. Laser Engraving

Laser engraving removes material from wood, metal, glass, or leather to create permanent marks. It produces elegant, precise results ideal for keepsakes, jewelry, cutting boards, and awards. The engraved look adds sophistication that printed text cannot match.

6. UV Printing

UV printing uses ultraviolet light to cure ink on virtually any surface. It works on wood, glass, acrylic, metal, and plastic. UV printers can produce photo-quality results with raised textures and white ink options. This versatile method is growing in popularity for custom gifts.

How to Choose the Right Method

Consider your product material, design complexity, order quantity, and budget. For photo-quality prints on fabric, choose DTG. For all-over prints on mugs and polyester, choose sublimation. For bulk orders with simple designs, screen printing wins. For premium keepsakes, laser engraving delivers unmatched quality.

Quality Tips

Always use high-resolution source files (300 DPI minimum). Test print a sample before committing to large batches. Ask your provider about color accuracy and wash durability. The right combination of print method and product material ensures your custom gifts look professional and last for years.

Method by Material and Order Size

Print methods are usually explained one at a time, which makes them hard to compare. The practical way to choose is to start from two facts: what the item is made of, and how many you need. Those two answers eliminate most of the list before you ever look at the artwork.

The table below maps common materials to the methods that work, the method to avoid, and the order size where each pairing makes sense.

Read the avoid column carefully, because it is where the expensive mistakes live. Most failures come from a material and method mismatch rather than from a bad design: sublimation on natural cotton, screen printing on a photographic gradient, or a heat-applied film on a curved insulated surface. Each of those pairings fails in a predictable way.

When a product looks like a borderline case, ask the provider rather than assuming. Coated blends, dark polyester, and drinkware with irregular shapes all sit between categories, and the answer depends on the specific blank rather than on the general rule.

Six Questions Before You Approve a Method

A method that works on a sample can still fail across a batch, and the six questions below catch the difference. They take about five minutes and apply to any product category.

The wash question is the one buyers skip, and it matters most for apparel and drinkware. A print that looks perfect in the sample can be destroyed by a hot dishwasher or a hot dryer, so the care routine is effectively part of the method decision. If the item will be washed hard, choose a process that tolerates it.

The quantity question also changes the answer more than people expect. A design with four colors and a gradient is perfectly reasonable for twenty shirts printed one way and prohibitively expensive at two hundred printed another. Decide quantity before you finalize the artwork, not after.

  • Does the blank material accept this process at full coverage?
  • How many colors and gradients does the artwork contain?
  • What is the real order quantity, including spares and reprints?
  • How will the item be washed or cleaned in normal use?
  • What does a physical sample look like in daylight?
  • What is the lead time for the chosen method at this quantity?

Design Files That Print Cleanly

The most common production problem is not the method but the file. Vector artwork scales to any size without losing edges, which is why logos and type should be supplied as vectors whenever possible, while photographs need enough pixels to print at final size, generally around 300 DPI.

Transparency is the second trap. A file with a transparent background can produce a black box in some workflows and a clean edge in others, so flatten your design onto the intended product color before uploading. Sending a design without a background also gives the printer no reference for how the white areas should behave.

Halftones and gradients need their own check. Screen printing approximates gradients with dots, and a subtle gradient may print as visible steps. If the design depends on smooth shading, choose a process that holds it, or replace the gradient with flat color bands that print predictably.

Cost and Lead Time by Method

Cost structure differs more than price does. Screen printing carries a per-color setup cost that spreads across the run, so it is expensive for small quantities and economical for large ones. DTG has no setup and charges per piece, which suits small orders and costs more per item as the quantity grows.

Sublimation and UV printing sit in the middle: minimal setup, moderate per-piece cost, and special requirements for the blank. Laser engraving is slow per item, since the machine traces each design, but the result is permanent and needs no curing, coating, or drying time.

Lead time follows the same logic. Methods with setup add time before production, engraving adds time per piece, and apparel methods add the most time when an order crosses into a new size run. Ordering three weeks ahead covers nearly every case in our catalog, including custom t-shirts and custom blankets.

Ask for the quote in the format you will actually order. A per-piece price at quantity one and a per-piece price at quantity two hundred describe two different processes, and comparing them directly is how buyers end up surprised.

Matching a Method to the Gift's Job

The final filter is use. An everyday item deserves a method that tolerates washing and handling, which usually means sublimation on drinkware and DTG or screen printing on apparel. A keepsake can use a method chosen purely for appearance, because it is handled rarely and stored more carefully.

Gifts that will be photographed matter too. Sublimated drinkware and DTG apparel both photograph well because the print follows the surface, while thick screen prints and raised vinyl reflect light and can look uneven in a phone photo. If the gift is going to be shared online, a flat print usually presents better.

And consider how the item will be replaced. Personalized items are usually ordered once, so a method that survives a decade of normal use is worth a small premium over the cheapest option, especially when the design depends on a photo that cannot be recreated later.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which print method is best for custom t-shirts?

DTG for detailed, multicolor designs on cotton in small quantities; screen printing for the same design in bulk. Sublimation only works on polyester garments.

Why does sublimation not work on cotton?

Sublimation dyes bond with polyester fibers at high heat. On cotton the dye sits on the surface and washes out, which is why sublimation products are always poly-blend or coated.
