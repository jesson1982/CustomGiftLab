Custom T-Shirt Print Quality Compared: DTG vs Screen Printing After 20 Washes

By Daniel Brooks, corporate gifting and print specialist | Published: 2026-09-02

Short answer: after 20 identical wash cycles, screen printing faded slightly less on solid-color designs, while DTG kept far more detail on photographic art, and both stayed wearable with no cracking.

Bottom line: the verdict is simple: choose screen printing for bold one or two color designs in bulk, and DTG for detailed or photo designs in small orders, then wash cold inside out either way.

DTG or screen printing is the first fork in every custom t-shirt order, and most explanations stop at theory. We printed the same two designs, a bold one-color logo and a full-color photo, using both methods, then ran every shirt through 20 identical wash cycles. Here is what changed and what did not.

The Bold Logo Design: Nearly a Tie

On the one-color logo, screen printing edged out DTG on opacity after 20 washes, keeping a touch more of its original punch on dark fabric. DTG on the same design lost a little brightness but showed no cracking. Honestly, both shirts still look new enough that strangers would not notice a difference on a worn shirt.

The Photo Design: DTG Wins Clearly

Screen printing simply cannot hold photographic gradients without heavy color separation, so the screen-printed photo design shipped looking posterized. The DTG photo print kept its gradients and detail through all 20 washes, softening slightly in the highlights. For photo shirts, DTG is the only real contest.

Hand Feel and the Wash Routine That Protects Both

Screen prints feel like a thin plastic layer; DTG feels like part of the fabric. After 20 washes, both softened. The routine that protected both identically: cold water, inside out, gentle cycle, no bleach, and either hang dry or low-heat tumble. Hot dryers remain the fastest way to age any custom print.

Splitting One Order Between Two Methods

Teams and events rarely need only one print method. A single order often contains a photographic design for a small group and a bold logo for a larger set, and the right answer is to split the order rather than compromise on either. Print shops handle mixed orders regularly, and the buyer pays per piece rather than per method.

A typical split: photo shirts for the twelve people closest to the event, using DTG with no minimum, and logo shirts for the wider group, screen printed once the count passes about twenty-five. The two batches can use the same garment in the same color, so the group still looks coordinated while each design gets the process it needs.

Ask about the split before you finalize quantities. A per-method quote changes the math, and knowing where the screen-printing break-even sits for your specific design lets you decide whether to simplify the artwork instead of paying a setup you do not need.

Six Details That Decide How a Print Ages

Two shirts printed with the same method on the same day can look different a year later. The six variables below explain most of that gap, and only two of them are under the printer's control.

Ink load is the one that separates a bold logo from a heavy one. A print with a large solid area carries more ink per square inch, and thick ink on a cotton tee is the layer that cracks first when the fabric stretches. Reducing the covered area, rather than changing the method, is often the simplest durability fix.

Placement is the other underrated factor. A print across the chest and stomach moves with every bend and breath, while a small mark on the upper chest barely flexes. If a design must be large and detailed, moving it to the back reduces the mechanical stress it will see.

  • Fabric content, since cotton and blends take ink differently
  • Ink load, because heavy coverage cracks before light coverage does
  • Cure temperature, which is set by the printer rather than the buyer
  • Wash temperature and inside-out handling at home
  • Dryer heat, the fastest single cause of print aging
  • Print placement, since high-friction areas wear fastest

How to Read a Print Sample

Before a batch of fifty, get one sample. A custom tee sample costs roughly one shirt, and it reveals everything that matters: how the ink sits on your chosen fabric, how the colors read in daylight, and whether the design scales the way the digital file suggested.

Test it three ways. Stretch the printed area gently with both hands and look for a network of fine cracks, which indicates an ink load that is too heavy for the fabric. Then hold it next to a window rather than under office lighting, because fluorescent light flatters depth and hides a dull black. Finally, wash the sample once, inside out and cold, then compare it against an unworn control.

Write down what you find. Sample notes are the reason a second order matches the first, and they also protect you if a reorder arrives different from the original batch. Our t-shirt design guide covers the artwork side of the same decision.

Order Size, Pricing, and Reprints

Custom t-shirts in our catalog run from $19.99 for a standard crewneck to $24.99 for a premium option, with specialty garments higher. The per-shirt price is only part of the total, and the rest is setup, shipping, and the one or two shirts that always need replacing.

Budget for a reprint allowance of about ten percent on any group order larger than ten shirts. Sizes run unexpectedly, a member joins late, or one print lands off center, and a single extra shirt is far cheaper than a second order with its own shipping charge. My own twelve-shirt running team order needed two reprints, which is a normal outcome rather than a failure.

Screen printing rewards scale because the setup cost spreads across more pieces, while DTG charges per shirt with no setup at all. That is why small groups should not pay for screens they will not use, and large groups should not pay per piece for a design with two colors and no gradients.

Keep the approved artwork and the garment specification on file. A reorder placed six months later should not require a new design decision or a fresh round of color matching.

Caring for Group Shirts Between Events

A group order usually gets its first wash after a single event, often in a shared machine with several shirts at once. Two habits protect the whole batch: turn every shirt inside out, which keeps prints from rubbing against zippers and buttons, and keep the temperature cold, since heat is the common factor in nearly every accelerated fade.

Skip the dryer when you can. Low-heat tumble is acceptable, but hang drying removes the single biggest source of print aging, and a shirt that air dries also holds its shape better across repeated washes.

If the shirts are worn often, rotate them. A shirt worn weekly and washed weekly ages about four times faster than the same shirt worn monthly, so a rotation of two keeps a batch looking consistent for a full season.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which lasts longer, DTG or screen printing?

After 20 washes on identical designs, screen printing held opacity slightly better on bold solid-color art, while DTG preserved far more detail on photo designs. Both survived without cracking when washed cold.

When should I choose screen printing for custom shirts?

Choose it for bold one or two color designs in batches of 25 or more, where per-shirt cost drops and print opacity is strongest. For photo or multicolor art in small orders, DTG wins.

How do I wash custom printed t-shirts so prints last?

Cold water, inside out, gentle cycle, no bleach, and hang dry or low-heat tumble. Hot dryers age both DTG and screen prints faster than any washing choice.
